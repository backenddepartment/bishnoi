# Ch. 4 — Khejarli, 1730

## Established — grade A

| | |
|---|---|
| **When** | September 1730; sources give 11 September, the date now marked nationally |
| **Where** | The village of Jehnad, near Jodhpur, afterwards known as Khejarli |
| **Under whom** | Maharaja Abhai Singh of Marwar |
| **Who led the party** | Giridhar Bhandari, a minister of the state |
| **Trees** | Khejri (*Prosopis cineraria*) |
| **Toll** | 363 Bishnois |
| **First to die** | Amrita Devi, then three of her daughters |
| **Aftermath** | Abhai Singh recalled his men and travelled to the village to apologise |
| **Now** | 11 September is National Forest Martyrs Day, declared 2013, explicitly in memory of Khejarli |

Environmental historians routinely cite Khejarli as a forerunner of the
twentieth-century Chipko movement, which used the same method of embracing trees
more than two centuries later.

## Hedged — grade B/C

- **Why the timber was wanted.** Sources split between a new palace and
  lime-burning kilns. Wikipedia carries both, attributed. The page should too;
  "for a royal building project" covers it without choosing.
- **How many villages.** Wikipedia says Bishnois came from **83** villages;
  coaching and community sources say **49**. Unresolved. Do not print a number
  until it is.
- **The decree.** See `claims-register.md` item 1. The narrow version — Khejarli
  exempted from supplying wood — is what the record supports. The broader version
  the site currently prints is tradition.

## The thing the page has to do

The audit's sharpest note on this material: *don't tell me 363 people died. Make
me understand why they did.*

The statistic is the least interesting part. What makes the event land is that it
was **not** a spontaneous protest — it was a decision, taken in sequence, with
time to reconsider at every step:

1. A tree was worth protecting. The rule says: do not cut a green tree.
2. Amrita Devi refused. The soldiers offered a bribe; she treated the offer as
   the insult.
3. She was killed. Then her daughters, who had watched it happen.
4. Word travelled to the surrounding villages. People came — knowing exactly what
   had already happened.
5. A council decided each volunteer would take one tree.
6. The elders went first, deliberately, before the young.
7. 363 died before the Maharaja stopped it.

Step 4 is the story. Everyone who died after Amrita Devi **chose to**, with full
knowledge. That is the difference between a massacre and a demonstration of
conviction, and it is what the current single-paragraph treatment loses.

Step 6 deserves its own sentence. Sending the old first is not a detail; it is the
community deciding how to spend its own lives.

## What it demonstrates

The reason this belongs on a page about a philosophy rather than a page about
trees: Khejarli is the evidence that the 29 niyamas were not advisory. They
determined what a person would defend, what a community would spend itself on,
and where the line between human life and the rest of the living world was
understood to fall.

That is the historical significance. "First eco-warriors" is not.

## Open questions

1. **83 or 49 villages.**
2. **The copper-plate decree** — if it survives in Marwar state records, this
   becomes the strongest documented fact on the site.
3. **Contemporary evidence versus later retelling.** Everything above comes from
   accounts written well after 1730. What, if anything, is contemporary? The
   reported 2026 Rajasthan High Court judgment reciting the narrative would be a
   useful non-community citation even though it is not contemporary evidence.
4. **The statement attributed to Amrita Devi** — "a head for a tree", in its
   various renderings. Almost certainly later tradition. Attribute it as such.
